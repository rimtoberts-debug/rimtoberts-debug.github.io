Bias, Fairness
and Ethics in AI

Explore where unfairness in AI comes from, how to spot it, and why rules and human oversight matter.

🎓GCSE & A-Level✓Free🧩simulations + quiz
Hook

A company trains a hiring tool on ten years of its past hiring decisions. The tool learns that people who look like previous hires are 'good', and quietly repeats old habits.

Nobody told it to be unfair. It simply learned what the data showed.

Think: Who should be responsible when an AI system treats people unfairly?
Explanation

Where bias comes from

Models learn from data, so biased data gives biased results. Data may leave out certain groups, reflect past prejudice, or be labelled carelessly.

Bias can also come from design choices, such as which features are used or what 'success' is measured as. A model can be accurate overall yet perform much worse for one group.

Explanation

Doing something about it

Teams can check performance separately for different groups, gather more representative data, remove features that act as stand-ins for protected traits, and keep a human in the loop for important decisions.

Other ethical questions include privacy (is personal data used fairly?), transparency (can the decision be explained?), and accountability. Some regions now use laws that treat higher-risk AI uses more strictly than low-risk ones.

Explanation

Types of bias to look for

Historical bias: the world the data came from was already unfair, and the model copies it. Sampling bias: some groups are missing or rare in the data. Measurement bias: the label or feature is recorded unevenly, such as arrests standing in for crime. Feedback loops: model outputs change the world and so the next batch of data, amplifying the problem.

Explanation

Fairness is a choice, not a switch

There are several reasonable definitions of fair: equal accuracy across groups, equal false-positive rates, equal approval rates. They cannot all be satisfied at once when groups differ, so people must decide which matters most for the situation and justify it openly.

Explanation

Responsibilities beyond bias

Privacy: collect only what is needed, store it safely and respect consent. Safety and security: models can be fooled by deliberately altered inputs. Environmental cost: training large models uses a lot of energy. Jobs and society: automation changes the work people do. Good practice includes impact assessments, documentation of what a model was trained on, and a clear route for people to challenge decisions.

Explanation

Transparency, explainability and accountability (A4.4)

Transparency means being open that AI is used and about what data and design choices shaped it. Explainability is the ability to give a human-understandable reason for a specific decision. Simple models such as small decision trees and linear models are naturally explainable; large neural networks are often called black boxes, so extra tools are used to estimate which inputs mattered.

Accountability asks who is responsible when an automated decision causes harm: the developer, the organisation deploying it, or the person who approved its use. Good practice keeps records of training data, testing and updates, and gives affected people a way to appeal to a human.

Explanation

Misuse, security and social impact

AI can generate convincing fake images, audio and video (deepfakes) and large volumes of misleading text, which affects trust in information and elections. Models can also be attacked, for example by adversarial inputs: tiny, deliberate changes that cause a wrong prediction, or by data poisoning of the training set.

Wider questions include surveillance and privacy, automation of jobs, unequal access to the technology (the digital divide), intellectual property of training data, and the energy use of training large models. Evaluating an AI system means weighing benefits against these costs for different stakeholders.

Simulation

Rate the risk

Many laws take a risk-based approach: the more harm an AI use could do, the stricter the rules. Place each use into a tier. (A simplified teaching model, not legal advice; real laws have detailed exceptions.)

Banned = unacceptable harm. High = strict testing and human oversight. Transparency = users must be told it is AI. Minimal = no special rules.

Bias lab: who is in the training data?

Two groups (A and B) have different patterns in the same measurement, so the best cut-off for A is not the best for B. A single model trained on pooled data fits whichever group dominates. Slide the share of group B in the training data and compare how well each group is served.

Explainability: why was I rejected?

A transparent model lets you see how each factor pushed the decision, and what would change it. Move the sliders for a loan applicant.

Score = 0.04×income − 0.08×debt + 0.15×years − 1. Approve if score > 0. A deep neural network with millions of weights could not be explained this directly, which is why explainable AI is an active research area.

Summary

What to remember

  • Biased or unrepresentative data produces biased models.
  • Design choices can add bias too.
  • Check results across different groups.
  • Privacy, transparency and accountability are key ethical issues.

Exam practice

Exam-style practice

Describe one way an AI system could become biased and one step to reduce it. [2 marks]

  • Trained on unrepresentative/prejudiced data so it favours some groups (1)
  • Collect more representative data / test across groups / human oversight (1)

A police force uses past arrest data to decide where to send patrols. Explain how this could create a feedback loop. [3 marks]

  • Patrols go where past arrests were recorded (1)
  • More patrols there find more incidents, adding more arrests in that area (1)
  • The model then sends even more patrols, reinforcing the original bias (1)
