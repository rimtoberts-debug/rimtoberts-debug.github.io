Neural Networks:
the Basics

Build up a neural network from a single artificial neuron, and see how weights and layers let it recognise patterns.

🎓GCSE & A-Level✓Free🧩simulations + quiz
Hook

Your brain contains billions of nerve cells passing tiny signals to each other. Early AI researchers wondered whether a much simpler imitation could learn too.

Today's image, speech and language systems are built on that idea.

Think: If one neuron is very simple, where might the power of a network come from?
Explanation

The artificial neuron

An artificial neuron takes several numbers as inputs. Each is multiplied by a weight that says how important it is. The results are added up, a bias is added, and an activation function decides how strongly the neuron 'fires'.

A tiny worked example: inputs 2 and 3 with weights 0.5 and 1 give 2×0.5 + 3×1 = 4. Adding a bias of −1 gives 3.

Explanation

Layers and learning

Neurons are arranged in layers: an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Each layer's outputs become the next layer's inputs.

Training adjusts the weights. After each prediction the error is measured and the weights are nudged to reduce it. With enough examples, useful patterns emerge. A network with many hidden layers is called deep learning.

Explanation

Activation functions: why not just add?

If every neuron only added up weighted inputs, stacking layers would collapse into one big weighted sum: no more powerful than a single neuron. An activation function bends the output so layers can build up complicated patterns.

Step gives a hard 0 or 1. Sigmoid squashes any number smoothly into 0 to 1. ReLU outputs zero for negative inputs and passes positive ones through, and is very common in deep networks because it is simple and trains well.

Explanation

How the weights get adjusted

After a forward pass the network compares its output with the target using a loss, a single number for how wrong it was. Backpropagation works backwards through the layers to find how much each weight contributed to that error.

Gradient descent then nudges each weight a small step in the direction that lowers the loss. The step size is the learning rate: too small and training crawls, too large and it overshoots and never settles.

Explanation

What a network 'sees'

In an image network, early layers tend to respond to simple things like edges, middle layers to shapes and textures, and later layers to whole objects. Nobody programs these roles: they emerge because they reduce the loss.

The price is opacity. A network may hold millions of weights, so explaining exactly why it decided something is hard. This is a major reason for work on explainable AI.

Questions worth thinking about

The weights (and biases) are adjusted to reduce the error on the training examples.
They let the network combine simple patterns into more complex ones, such as edges into shapes into objects.
Summary

What to remember

  • A neuron: inputs × weights, plus a bias, then an activation.
  • Networks have input, hidden and output layers.
  • Training adjusts weights to reduce error.
  • Many hidden layers is called deep learning.
