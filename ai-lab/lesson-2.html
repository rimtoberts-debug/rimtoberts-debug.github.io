How Machines
Learn from Data

Meet the vocabulary of machine learning: features, labels, training and testing, and what overfitting looks like.

🎓GCSE & A-Level✓Free🧩simulations + quiz
Hook

Imagine teaching a younger sibling to spot ripe bananas by showing them hundreds of examples, 'ripe' or 'not ripe'. You never state a rule, yet they get good at it.

Machine learning mirrors that: lots of labelled examples in, a pattern out.

Think: What would go wrong if every banana you showed them was from the same shop?
Explanation

The vocabulary

Features are the measurable inputs about each example, such as a banana's colour and softness. The label is the answer we want predicted, such as 'ripe'.

A training set is the data the model learns from. A separate test set is held back and used afterwards to check how well the model copes with examples it has never seen.

Explanation

Fitting, and fitting too well

A good model captures the general pattern. A model that overfits memorises the quirks and noise of the training data, scoring brilliantly in training but poorly on new data.

An underfit model is too simple to capture the pattern at all and does badly everywhere. The aim is the balance between them.

More varied, good-quality data usually helps. Data that is small, messy or one-sided leads to a weak model.

Explanation

Features: choosing what the model sees

A model can only use what you give it. Good features are relevant to the answer, measurable and not mostly noise. Predicting if a banana is ripe from its colour works; predicting it from the shop's opening hours does not.

Often numbers must be put on a similar scale first (a height in metres and a salary in pounds would otherwise swamp each other). This is called normalising the data.

Explanation

Measuring how good a model is

Accuracy is the percentage of predictions that are right. It can mislead on lopsided data: a model that always says 'no fraud' is 99.9% accurate if only 0.1% of payments are fraudulent, yet useless.

This is why we also look at which kinds of mistake are made (see lesson 3) and why test data should resemble the real situation the model will face.

Explanation

Fixing under- and overfitting

To reduce overfitting: gather more data, use a simpler model, remove noisy features, or stop training sooner. To reduce underfitting: use a more flexible model or add informative features.

In practice data is often split three ways: training to learn, validation to tune settings such as k, and test for one final honest score.

Explanation

Regression and common algorithms

Linear regression fits the straight line that minimises the squared errors, and is the simplest way to predict a number. k-nearest neighbours, decision trees, logistic regression and neural networks are common supervised choices for classification. k-means is a standard unsupervised clustering method. Choosing between them depends on the data size, whether labels exist, and whether the decision must be explainable.

Summary

What to remember

  • Features are inputs; the label is the answer to predict.
  • Train on one set, test on another.
  • Overfitting memorises noise; underfitting misses the pattern.
  • Better, more varied data gives better models.

Exam practice

Exam-style practice

A student trains a model and tests it on the same data it learned from. Explain why the result may be misleading. [2 marks]

  • The model may have memorised the training data (1)
  • So the score does not show how well it works on new, unseen data (1)

A model for detecting a rare disease is 98% accurate but only 2% of patients have the disease. Explain why this result may not show the model is good. [3 marks]

  • A model that always predicts 'no disease' would also score 98% (1)
  • Accuracy hides that it misses every person who is ill (1)
  • Need to look at false negatives / other measures too (1)
