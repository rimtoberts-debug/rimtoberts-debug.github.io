Large Language Models:
How do chatbots work?

Learn how text is split into tokens, how a model predicts the next token, and why confident answers can still be wrong.

🎓GCSE & A-Level✓Free🧩simulations + quiz
Hook

Type half a sentence into your phone and it suggests the next word. Now imagine that idea scaled up enormously, trained on a huge amount of text, and able to continue with whole paragraphs.

That is the core of a large language model.

Think: If a model only predicts what text is likely next, does it 'know' that its answer is true?
Explanation

Tokens and prediction

Text is split into small pieces called tokens, which may be whole words, parts of words or punctuation. Each token is turned into numbers the network can process.

A large language model (LLM) is trained to predict the most likely next token given the tokens so far. Repeating this one token at a time produces sentences, code or summaries.

Explanation

Strengths and limits

Because they learn patterns from vast amounts of text, LLMs write fluently, translate, and answer many questions well.

But they predict plausible text, not verified facts. A hallucination is a confident-sounding answer that is false or invented. They can also repeat biases from their training text, and they know nothing newer than their training data unless given tools.

Good practice is to check important claims against reliable sources, and not to share private information with a chatbot.

Explanation

Why transformers changed everything

Earlier language models read words one after another and struggled to remember the start of a long sentence. The transformer design uses attention: for each token it learns how much to focus on every other token. In 'The trophy would not fit in the case because it was too big', attention helps link 'it' to 'trophy'.

Because attention can be computed for many tokens in parallel, transformers can be trained on enormous amounts of text using many processors at once.

Explanation

From predictor to assistant

A model trained only to continue text is called a base model. To make a helpful assistant, developers further train it on example conversations and use human ratings of its answers to steer it towards responses people judge as helpful, honest and safe (often called fine-tuning with human feedback).

Settings such as temperature control how adventurous the sampling is. A context window limits how much text the model can consider at once.

Explanation

Using chatbots well

Treat output as a first draft. Verify facts and quotations, check any code before running it, be aware that the model can echo biases in its training text, and do not paste in personal, confidential or exam-restricted material. Being clear about what you want (the task, the audience, the format) usually gives noticeably better results: this skill is called prompting.

Summary

What to remember

  • Text is broken into tokens.
  • An LLM predicts the next token, repeatedly.
  • Fluent does not mean correct; hallucinations happen.
  • Verify important information and protect private data.
